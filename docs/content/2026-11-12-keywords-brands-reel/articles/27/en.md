Matted fur, nails clicking on the floor or a smell that no bath seems to fix usually mean it's time for dog grooming. Grooming isn't just about looks: regular care of the coat, nails and ears prevents painful mats, sores and infections, and gives someone a close look at your dog's skin. This guide explains what a good groomer does, how to choose one, how often each coat type needs attention and what you can safely do at home. ## What does a dog grooming appointment include?

A full groom usually follows these steps:

- **Pre-brush:** mats and loose hair come out before the bath, because water tightens mats.
- **Bath:** dog shampoo, lukewarm water and a thorough rinse so no residue stays on the skin.
- **Drying:** towel and a dryer on a gentle temperature; a thick coat left damp invites skin irritation and infection.
- **De-shedding:** for double coats, special rakes and brushes remove dead undercoat that traps heat.
- **Haircut:** with clippers or scissors, to suit the breed and your preference.
- **Nails:** clipped or filed, with the hair between the paw pads tidied.
- **Ears:** the outer ear cleaned and checked for smell or discharge.
- **Sanitary trim:** hair around the bottom and genitals kept short for hygiene.

A good groomer tells you if they spot fleas, ticks, lumps, wounds or red skin, but diagnosis and treatment are your vet's job. Anaesthesia-free teeth scaling at a salon isn't recommended: it only cleans the visible surface and misses disease under the gumline.

## How to choose a good dog groomer

Visit before the first appointment and check or ask:

- **Sedation only by a vet:** a groomer who gives a dog tablets or injections to calm it puts the dog at risk. If your dog can't be groomed without sedation, that belongs under veterinary supervision.
- **Calm handling:** patience and short breaks, not force, shouting or long periods of restraint.
- **Clean tools:** tables, scissors, clipper blades and brushes are cleaned and disinfected between dogs, since dirty tools can spread skin infections.
- **Dryer heat:** a dog is never left alone in a heated cage dryer. Flat-faced breeds such as pugs and shih tzus, and older dogs, overheat quickly.
- **Honesty about mats:** with a badly matted coat, a good groomer suggests clipping short rather than painful dematting, and explains why.
- **Vaccinations:** ask whether they require vaccination records, since many dogs pass through the same space.
- **Transparency:** you can see the work area and know in advance what's included.

### Is mobile dog grooming a good idea?

A mobile groomer comes to your home or parks a fitted van outside. For anxious or older dogs, or dogs that get carsick, skipping the journey and the other dogs is a real plus. The same standards apply: clean tools, calm handling, full drying and no sedation without a vet.

## How often should you bathe and groom a dog?

There's no single number. For most dogs, a bath every one to three months, or whenever they're dirty or smelly, is enough. Frequent baths with the wrong product dry the skin, and human shampoo is never suitable. After city walks, wiping paws with a damp cloth often beats another bath. If your vet has prescribed medicated baths, follow their schedule.

### Grooming schedule by coat type

- **Short, smooth coats** (boxer, Dobermann): a weekly brush or grooming mitt; the groomer mainly for baths and nails.
- **Double coats** (husky, German shepherd, golden retriever): brushing several times a week and daily in shedding season with an undercoat rake; a professional de-shed a few times a year.
- **Long or curly coats that keep growing** (shih tzu, Maltese, Yorkshire terrier, poodle): daily or every-other-day brushing to prevent mats, and a professional trim roughly every 4 to 8 weeks.
- **Wire coats** (schnauzer, many terriers): weekly brushing and clipping or hand-stripping every few months.

Most dogs need their nails trimmed every few weeks; if you hear clicking on the floor, they're too long.

### Should you shave a double-coated dog in summer?

No. The undercoat works as insulation and, together with the outer guard hairs, protects the skin from heat and sun. Shaving leaves the skin open to sunburn and irritation, and the coat sometimes grows back patchy. Brush out the dead undercoat regularly instead, offer shade and cool water, and walk in the cooler hours. The exception is severe matting, where clipping short may be kinder to the dog.

## Grooming your dog at home between appointments

You can do much of the routine care yourself, which stretches the time between appointments:

1. From puppyhood, get your dog used to having paws, ears and mouth handled, rewarding each time with a treat.
2. Practise a "stay" so your dog stands calmly; our [basic dog commands](/en/blog/basic-dog-commands) guide shows how.
3. Brush on your coat type's schedule, right down to the skin, not just the surface.
4. Tease small tangles apart with fingers and a comb. Never cut a mat out with scissors close to the skin; cutting the skin under a mat is one of the most common home injuries.
5. Trim nails a little at a time to avoid the quick. If a nail bleeds, press with clean gauze for a few minutes and call your vet if it doesn't stop.
6. Tidy the hair between the pads with round-tipped scissors.
7. Clean only the outer ear, with cotton wool and a vet-recommended cleaner, and never push anything into the ear canal.
8. If you use clippers, keep testing the blade on your wrist; a hot blade burns skin.

Basic home kit: a slicker brush, a metal comb, an undercoat rake for double coats, dog nail clippers and dog shampoo. You'll find these in our [dog grooming supplies](/en/shop/products?category=grooming&species=DOG).

## When should a vet look at your dog's skin and coat?

While brushing or after a groom, book a vet visit if you notice:

- redness, flaking, pimples or bald patches
- constant scratching, paw licking or head shaking; see [dog itching causes](/en/blog/dog-itching-causes)
- fleas, flea dirt or ticks; our [flea and tick prevention](/en/blog/flea-tick-prevention) guide explains what to do
- a bad smell or discharge from the ears
- a new lump or bump under the skin
- unusual hair loss outside the shedding season

:::warning
The most harmful grooming mistakes are sedation without a vet, leaving a dog alone in a heated dryer, cutting mats with scissors close to the skin, using human shampoo and shaving a double coat. If your dog pants heavily, drools, seems weak or collapses during or after grooming, it may be overheating: move it somewhere cool, wet it with cool (not ice-cold) water and go to a vet immediately. Bleeding that won't stop, a deep cut, or painful bumps with fever or lethargy a day or two after a bath also need a vet the same day.
:::

## How much does dog grooming cost?

Prices vary by place and change over time, so look at what drives the cost:

- **Size:** bigger dogs take more time and shampoo.
- **Coat type and condition:** long, double or matted coats take longer.
- **Service:** bath only, bath and brush, or a full groom with a haircut; nails and ears are sometimes extra.
- **Style:** breed-specific cuts and detailed scissoring take more time.
- **Temperament:** an anxious dog needs a slower, calmer session.
- **Mobile or salon:** home visits are often priced differently.
- **Location.**

Ask what's included before you book. Regular brushing at home usually saves you the cost of dematting later.

## Dog grooming: frequently asked questions

:::faq
? How often should I bathe my dog?
For most dogs, every one to three months or whenever they're dirty or smelly. Always use dog shampoo and dry your dog completely.
? Is it safe to groom my dog at home?
Brushing, nail trims and tidying paw hair are fine at home. Cutting mats with scissors near the skin or using clippers without experience can cut or burn the skin, so leave full haircuts to a groomer.
? How much does a dog groomer cost?
It depends on size, coat type and condition, the services and style, temperament, mobile or salon, and location. Ask what's included before booking.
? Should I shave my husky in summer?
No. The double coat protects the skin from heat and sun. Brush out the dead undercoat and provide shade, cool water and walks in the cooler hours.
? Can a groomer sedate my dog?
No. Sedation should only be decided on and given by a vet, because it can affect breathing and body temperature.
:::
