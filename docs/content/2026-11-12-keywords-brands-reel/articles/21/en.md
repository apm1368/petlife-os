It's 2 a.m., your cat has vomited five times or your dog suddenly can't stand. The question is always the same: do we need a 24-hour emergency vet right now? This isn't a symptom encyclopedia but a calm decision guide: the signs that can't wait, what to say on the phone, safe transport, what to bring and when an online vet is enough. ## Which signs can't wait until morning?

If you see any of these, go now. A few hours' delay can change the outcome.

- **Breathing trouble:** open-mouth breathing in a cat, wheezing, heaving with the belly, or blue, grey or very pale gums.
- **Seizures:** one lasting over five minutes, several in a row, or a first-ever seizure.
- **Collapse:** unable to stand, unresponsive, or sudden paralysis of the hind legs.
- **Bleeding:** bleeding that doesn't stop with firm pressure, blood in vomit or urine, or black, tarry stool.
- **Trauma:** a road accident, a fall from a balcony or window, or bite wounds. Internal injuries can take hours to show.
- **Possible poisoning:** chocolate, xylitol, grapes and raisins, rat poison, antifreeze, any human medicine, and for cats any part of a lily, even pollen or vase water. See our list of [toxic foods for dogs](/en/blog/toxic-foods-for-dogs).
- **A male cat who can't pee:** repeated trips to the litter box, straining, producing nothing or a few drops. A urinary blockage can become fatal within a day or two.
- **A bloated dog:** a hard, swollen belly with unproductive retching and drooling, especially in large, deep-chested breeds. With a twisted stomach, minutes count.
- **Heatstroke:** heavy panting, drooling and wobbliness after heat or time in a car.
- **Severe vomiting or diarrhea:** repeated, bloody, with lethargy, or in a puppy, kitten or senior.
- **Difficult birth:** strong straining for more than 20 to 30 minutes with no puppy or kitten born.

### What can usually wait until morning?

In an otherwise bright adult, a single vomit, mild diarrhea without blood or a slight limp can usually wait for a morning call to your regular vet. To judge vomiting, read [why cats vomit](/en/blog/cat-vomiting-causes); for loose stools, see [dog diarrhea](/en/blog/dog-diarrhea-causes). If things get worse, the animal is very young or old, or you're unsure, call.

:::warning
**Don't "wait and see" with these:** breathing difficulty, long or repeated seizures, collapse, heavy bleeding, any poison swallowed, a male cat who can't pee, a bloated belly with dry retching, trauma or heatstroke. On the way, don't give human medicines, never make your pet vomit with salt water or anything else unless a vet tells you to, and never pull a string hanging from a cat's mouth or bottom.
:::

## Before you leave: a step-by-step plan

[[INFOGRAPHIC]]

Ten calm minutes beat a frantic drive to a clinic with no vet on duty tonight.

1. **Stay calm and protect yourself.** A pet in pain may bite, even the gentlest one. Keep your face away from theirs.
2. **Reduce the immediate danger.** Press a clean cloth firmly on a bleeding wound, adding layers rather than lifting it. Cool an overheated pet with cool, not ice-cold, water. During a seizure, keep hands away from the mouth and time it.
3. **Call the clinic.** Check that a vet is on site now and can take this case.
4. **Give nothing by mouth.** No medicine, food or milk unless the vet says so; your pet may need anesthesia.
5. **Grab your things and go.** If someone is with you, let them drive while you sit with your pet.

### What to say when you call ahead

Keep these lines in a note on your phone:

- Species, age and rough weight: "Male dog, six years old, about 20 kilos."
- What happened and when: "Retching for half an hour, nothing comes up, belly is hard."
- If something was swallowed: the exact product, how much and when.
- Existing conditions and current medications.
- Ask: "What should we do until we arrive?"

Some clinics open late but can't do overnight surgery or oxygen therapy, so one call can save a trip to the wrong place.

## How to get your pet safely to a 24-hour emergency vet

A frightened or injured animal can escape, get hurt further or distract the driver.

- **Cats always travel in a carrier.** No carrier? Wrap the cat in a towel and use a sturdy cardboard box with air holes. Never carry a cat loose in your arms.
- **Small dogs in a carrier, larger dogs on a leash and harness,** on the back seat or floor, never the front seat.
- **Keep injured pets flat.** After a fall or accident, move a large dog on a board or a blanket held at the corners by two people. Don't try to splint a fracture.
- **Muzzle only if needed,** and never if the dog is vomiting or struggling to breathe.
- **Mind the temperature.** Keep a cold, weak pet warm, but don't cover an overheated one; use the air conditioning. For tiny kittens, see safe warming in our [orphan kitten care](/en/blog/orphan-kitten-care) guide.

No car? Not every taxi or ride-hailing driver accepts animals. Check today whether a [pet taxi](/en/services/PET_TAXI) operates in your area and save its number; our [pet taxi guide](/en/blog/pet-taxi-guide) covers what to ask before booking. In a true emergency, though, take whatever safe transport arrives first.

## What to bring to the emergency clinic

- Vaccination and medical records, or photos of them.
- A list of medications and supplements, or the packs themselves.
- **The packaging of anything swallowed,** even if empty, and a piece of any chewed plant in a sealed bag.
- **A photo or small sample of vomit or stool** in a closed container.
- A note of times: last meal, when symptoms began, how long a seizure lasted. A short video of a seizure or the breathing pattern is very useful.
- Leash or carrier, a towel, an absorbent pad, a charged phone and a way to pay; many clinics give an estimate and may ask for a deposit before major treatment.

## When is an online vet enough?

A video call helps you decide whether to go in, but a vet on a screen can't feel the belly or run tests.

**Usually enough when:**

- your pet is bright and your question is "can this wait until morning?"
- there's one mild sign, such as a single vomit in an adult.
- you have a question about behavior, diet or a treatment your own vet started.

**Not enough when:**

- any red flag above is present; the call only alerts the clinic you're coming.
- your pet is in pain, lethargic or getting worse.
- it's a puppy, kitten, senior or chronically ill pet, or medication needs prescribing.

Free online vet advice in forums is fine for general questions, but never give a medicine a stranger suggests. Often the quickest free triage is calling the 24-hour clinic and asking whether to come in.

## Prepare today: find your nearest 24-hour vet

The closest clinic on the map isn't always the best one at midnight, and rural areas may have no overnight service nearby. Do this once:

1. **Find two clinics:** the nearest one truly open overnight, plus a backup.
2. **Call and ask:** is a vet on site at night or only on call? Do they treat dogs and cats? Can they hospitalize, give oxygen and operate overnight?
3. **Save the numbers** on every household phone and on paper by the door.
4. **Check the route,** parking and the night entrance.
5. **Pack an emergency kit:** carrier, towel, gauze and bandage, gloves, pads and copies of records.
6. **Know your pet's normal:** healthy gums are pink, and the sleeping breathing rate is usually under 30 breaths a minute.

Emergency costs depend on the time of day, tests, imaging, hospitalization and surgery; a small monthly savings pot for your pet makes that night's decisions easier.

It can help you judge urgency, but no one can diagnose without an exam. With any red flag, go straight to a clinic.

Don't give food, human medicines or salt water, don't induce vomiting unless a vet says so, and don't carry a cat outside a carrier.

It depends on the time of day, the exam, tests and imaging, fluids, oxygen, hospitalization and surgery. Ask for an estimate before major treatment.
