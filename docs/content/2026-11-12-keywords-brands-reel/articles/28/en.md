:::summary
A **pet taxi** is a ride service whose drivers accept dogs and cats and are set up to carry them: secure anchoring for carriers or harnesses, working air conditioning, washable seat covers and cleaning between rides. It's useful for trips to the vet, the groomer, boarding or the airport, or whenever you don't have a car. Cats should always travel in a closed carrier and dogs in a crate or a harness clipped to the seat belt; before booking, ask about carriers, cooling, cleaning and whether you can ride along.
:::

Vaccinations, check-ups, grooming, boarding: most pets need a ride several times a year. If you don't have a car, your dog is too big for a regular cab, or drivers won't take animals, a pet taxi is the simpler option. This guide covers what a pet taxi is, when it's the best choice, what to ask before you book, carrier training for cats and car safety for dogs. ## What is a pet taxi, and how is it different from a regular taxi?

Some taxi and ride-hailing drivers refuse animals, others accept only a cat in a carrier, and few are ready for a large dog. A pet taxi is set up for animals from the start. A good one usually offers:

- a driver who is comfortable with dogs and cats and knows how to keep a frightened animal calm;
- washable seat covers and a fixed spot to secure a carrier or harness;
- working air conditioning and ventilation, because a closed car heats up fast in summer;
- cleaning and disinfection between rides, especially after a sick animal;
- the option for the owner to ride along.

A pet taxi isn't a veterinary ambulance: the driver transports your pet but doesn't provide medical care.

## When does a pet taxi make sense?

- **Vet visits:** vaccinations, check-ups, tests, or the ride home after surgery while your pet is still drowsy.
- **Grooming:** especially for large or long-coated dogs. Still looking for a good groomer? Read our [dog grooming guide](/en/blog/dog-grooming-guide).
- **Boarding:** drop-off before your trip and pick-up afterwards; our [dog and cat boarding guide](/en/blog/pet-boarding-guide) helps you choose the place.
- **Airport or station runs:** if your pet flies with you, a pet taxi gets you there with the carrier and luggage. Check the airline's rules early and plan the rest in our [pet travel section](/en/travel).
- **Moving house,** several pets at once, or **no car** that fits your dog.

## What to ask before booking a pet taxi

A five-minute call before you book prevents most problems on the day:

- **Carriers:** Do you provide one, or should I bring my own? What sizes? How is it secured in the car?
- **Large dogs:** Do you have a travel crate or a harness with a seat-belt attachment?
- **Cooling:** Does the air conditioning work? If we wait somewhere, does the car stay cool?
- **Cleaning:** Do you clean and disinfect seats and carriers between rides?
- **Shared rides:** Will my pet travel with animals from other households? The better answer is no, unless you agree to it.
- **Riding along:** Can I sit next to my pet? For cats and anxious dogs, your presence helps a lot.
- **If your pet travels alone:** Who hands them over and picks them up? Can I reach the driver directly? Will you send a message on arrival?
- **Waiting and return:** Will you wait at the clinic and bring us home?
- **Hours:** Do you drive at night or on public holidays?

The fare usually depends on distance, waiting time, return trips, night or holiday hours, the number and size of animals and whether a carrier is supplied. Ask what is included before you confirm.

## How to get your cat used to the carrier

Never carry a cat in your arms, in a bag or loose on the seat; one car horn is enough for them to bolt. Choose a sturdy carrier with a secure door, ideally with a removable top so the vet can examine your cat inside it, and one carrier per cat. To make the carrier a safe den rather than "the vet box", start days or weeks ahead:

1. **Make it furniture.** Leave the carrier open in a room your cat likes.
2. **Make it cozy.** Add a blanket or T-shirt that smells of home, and drop in treats or toys now and then.
3. **Move meals closer:** first next to the carrier, then at the door, then inside.
4. **Close the door for a few seconds,** then open it again, slowly building up the time.
5. **Lift and carry it around the house,** then take a short car ride around the block.
6. **On the day,** drape a light cloth over the carrier to calm your cat, without blocking the airflow.

For more on reward-based training, see our guide to [cat training basics](/en/blog/cat-training-basics).

## Car safety for dogs: crate or harness?

A loose dog can be thrown forward in a sudden stop and hurt themselves and passengers.

- **Small dogs:** in a carrier belted onto the back seat or placed on the floor behind the front seat.
- **Medium and large dogs:** a sturdy harness attached to the seat-belt buckle with a short tether, or a secured travel crate in the cargo area of a larger vehicle.
- **Right crate size:** your dog should be able to stand, turn around and lie down comfortably.

Never put a dog on the front seat or on the driver's lap, where an airbag can hurt them. Never clip the tether to the collar, which can injure the neck when braking. Don't let them hang their head out of the window, and never put a dog in the closed trunk of a car.

## Heat, stress and motion sickness on the way

**Heat:** a parked car heats up dangerously within minutes on a warm day, even with the windows cracked and in the shade. Cool the car before your pet gets in, and on longer trips bring water and a bowl.

**Stress:** panting, drooling, yawning, lip licking, whining or loud meowing are signs of stress. Talk calmly and ask for smooth driving. If a cat keeps breathing with an open mouth after the car stops and cools down, call your vet.

**Motion sickness:** puppies are more prone, and many grow out of it. Give only a light meal a few hours before the trip, put an absorbent pad in the carrier and start with short rides. If your pet is sick on every trip, ask your vet about prevention. If vomiting continues after the journey, read [why cats vomit](/en/blog/cat-vomiting-causes).

:::warning
**These mistakes can be deadly:** leaving a dog or cat in a parked car, even for a few minutes in the heat; carrying a cat without a carrier; a dog loose, on the front seat or in a closed trunk; clipping a tether to the collar; and setting off with a pet who is lethargic, struggling to breathe or vomiting repeatedly without calling a vet first. Heavy panting, staggering, or very red or bluish gums on the way mean go to the nearest vet now.
:::

## Travel day: what to pack

[[INFOGRAPHIC]]

- Carrier or harness and leash, with an ID tag showing your phone number.
- An absorbent pad in the carrier plus a spare, wipes and poop bags.
- A blanket or piece of clothing that smells of home.
- Water and a collapsible bowl for longer trips.
- Vaccination records; boarding kennels, groomers and airlines usually ask for them.
- Medicines and medical notes if your pet has a condition.
- A recent photo of your pet on your phone, in case they ever get loose.

## Pet taxi at night and in emergencies

If your pet falls ill at midnight, call the 24-hour clinic first, then arrange the ride. If you already have the number of a pet taxi that works at night, tell the driver it's an emergency and go straight to the clinic. Sit next to your pet, keep them flat and calm, and give nothing by mouth. If a pet taxi can't come quickly, don't wait; any safe vehicle that arrives first is better. Our [24-hour emergency vet guide](/en/blog/24-hour-vet-guide) lists the signs that can't wait and what to bring.

## Pet taxi FAQ

:::faq
? What is a pet taxi and how does it differ from a normal taxi?
Its drivers accept animals and its cars are set up for them: anchoring for carriers or harnesses, air conditioning, seat covers and cleaning between rides. A normal taxi may refuse animals.
? Does my cat have to travel in a carrier?
Yes. A frightened cat can leap out of your arms, hide under the pedals or bolt when the door opens. A sturdy, closed carrier is the safest way.
? Can I ride along in the pet taxi?
Usually yes, and it's better, especially for cats and anxious dogs. Confirm it when you book.
? How can I prevent motion sickness in my dog or cat?
Give only a light meal a few hours before, keep the car well ventilated, drive smoothly and build up with short trips. If it keeps happening, talk to your vet and never give human medicines.
? What does a pet taxi cost?
It depends on distance, waiting time, return trips, night hours, the number and size of pets and whether a carrier is provided.
:::
