Knowing **how to choose a dog trainer** comes down to a few checks: a good trainer uses reward-based methods, coaches you as well as your dog, sets clear goals and never guarantees results. Before you pay, ask about methods, equipment, experience and follow-up, and walk away from anyone who recommends shock, prong or choke collars or talks about "dominance". If your dog's behaviour has changed suddenly or it has become aggressive, see your vet first.

Learning how to choose a dog trainer matters. In many places anyone can call themselves a dog trainer, and a certificate alone does not tell you how someone works. The wrong choice costs more than money: harsh methods can make fear and aggression worse. This guide gives you the questions to ask, the red flags to watch for and a picture of what good training looks like. ## What makes a good dog trainer?

Reward-based training means reinforcing the behaviour you want with something your dog values, such as food, play or praise, so it happens more often. Unwanted behaviour is reduced by managing the environment and teaching an alternative, not by pain or fear: a dog that jumps on guests learns to sit by the door instead. Leading veterinary and animal behaviour organisations recommend this approach because it works and keeps your relationship with your dog healthy.

The old "pack leader" and "dominance" ideas have long been dropped by behaviour experts. Most problem behaviour comes from fear, boredom, gaps in training or pain, not from a dog trying to be the boss. Signs you have found a good trainer:

- They teach your dog what to do, not only what not to do.
- They involve your family and hand the lead to you during the session.
- They make things easier when your dog is stressed or tired.
- They are honest about their limits and refer you to a vet when needed.

## Group class, private sessions or in-home dog training?

None of them is best for everyone; it depends on your dog's age, your goals and the problem.

### Puppy classes and group classes

A puppy class is excellent for socialisation. Most trainers accept puppies after their first vaccination, with the vet's agreement. A good class keeps groups small, is clean, asks for vaccination records and keeps off-lead play short and supervised. Our [puppy training by age](/en/blog/puppy-training-by-age) guide shows what fits each stage, and if mouthing is your main worry, read about [puppy biting](/en/blog/puppy-biting). Group classes are not the place to start for a dog that is very fearful of, or reactive to, other dogs.

### Private and in-home sessions

If the problem is tied to your home, such as barking at the door, toilet accidents or jumping on visitors, in-home training is usually better: the trainer sees the real setting, from the lift to the neighbours.

### Board-and-train: think twice

Here your dog lives with the trainer for a few weeks. You cannot see the methods, your dog may respond to the trainer but not to you, and harsh handling is easier to hide. If you still choose it, visit, ask for daily videos and insist on handover sessions.

## How to choose a dog trainer: 10 questions to ask before you pay

[[INFOGRAPHIC]]

Note the answers and compare two or three trainers. Vague answers or "just trust me" are a signal in themselves.

1. What exactly do you do when a dog gets something wrong? Listen for management, alternatives and rewards, not "corrections" with the lead.
2. What equipment do you use? A flat collar or harness, a lead, treats and toys are enough.
3. What training have you had, and when did you last take a course?
4. Have you worked with dogs like mine, in age, size and problem?
5. What is my role in the sessions? The best answer: you are there and you practise.
6. What happens in the first session, and what will you ask about health and history?
7. How do you set goals and measure progress?
8. Roughly how many sessions should we expect, and how do you follow up between them?
9. What do you do if you see signs of pain, illness or serious aggression? The right answer is a referral to the vet.
10. Can I watch a class first, and what is your cancellation policy?

You can put the same questions to trainers listed under [dog training services](/en/services/TRAINING) before you book.

## Red flags: which dog trainers to avoid

If you notice any of these, keep looking:

- Using or recommending shock (e-collars), prong or choke collars, or sprays and sudden noises to startle the dog.
- Talk of "dominance", "being the alpha" or "showing your dog who's boss".
- Guaranteed results, or a promise to fix a problem in one session.
- Refusing to let you watch, or being vague about what they do with your dog.
- Pushing a frightened dog into what it fears until it "gets used to it".

:::warning
The mistakes that do the most harm: hitting, jerking the lead, shouting and painful tools such as shock, prong and choke collars. They increase fear, stress and the risk of biting, and can injure the neck and windpipe. A dog punished for growling may later bite without warning. If your dog has suddenly become aggressive or its behaviour has changed overnight, see the vet before any training; pain and illness are common causes.
:::

## What should the first session with a dog trainer look like?

You should leave with a clear plan, not just a tired dog. A good first session usually includes:

1. **A conversation:** the trainer asks about age, health, vaccinations, routine, behaviour history and your goals. Have vaccination records ready.
2. **Observation:** they watch your dog and let it approach in its own time, without forcing contact.
3. **A simple explanation:** why the behaviour probably happens and what makes it worse.
4. **One small skill:** they teach you an easy exercise and patiently coach your timing.
5. **Management and homework:** ways to stop the dog rehearsing the problem, such as a baby gate or a different walking time, plus a short written plan.

### What to practise at home between sessions

Most progress happens between sessions:

- Two to four sessions of 3 to 10 minutes a day rather than one long one at the weekend.
- Everyone at home uses the same cue words and rules.
- Send your trainer a short video so they can check your timing.
- Brush up the basics with our [basic dog commands](/en/blog/basic-dog-commands) guide.
- For common home problems, see [how to stop dog barking](/en/blog/stop-dog-barking) and [dog potty training](/en/blog/dog-potty-training).

## How much does a dog trainer cost? What the price depends on

Prices vary by place and over time, so here is what you are paying for instead of a number:

- **Format:** group classes split the cost; private sessions take the trainer's full time.
- **Location:** in-home training includes the trainer's travel time.
- **Length and number of sessions,** sold singly or as a package.
- **The problem:** fear or aggression usually needs more sessions than puppy basics.
- **Experience and support:** whether follow-up is included; board-and-train adds boarding costs.

Compare the total cost of reaching your goal, not the price of one session. Many basics you can teach yourself for free; what a good trainer adds is watching your timing, reading your dog and building a plan for your home.

## When you need a vet or a behaviourist instead

Some behaviour has a medical cause and should be checked before training:

- Sudden aggression or a sudden change in behaviour.
- Signs of pain: limping, avoiding touch in one area, difficulty sitting or using stairs.
- New toilet accidents in a house-trained dog.
- An older dog that seems confused or restless at night.
- Severe fear or separation distress with self-injury or refusing food.

Your vet can rule out pain and illness and refer you to a veterinary behaviourist if needed. You can [find a vet](/en/vet/find) near you on opetlife. Meanwhile, never punish growling.

## Frequently asked questions

:::faq
? Can I train my dog without paying a trainer?
Yes, most basics can be taught at home with short daily sessions. For fear, aggression or a problem that does not improve, a few sessions with a reward-based trainer are worth it.
? How much does a dog trainer cost?
It depends on the format, number and length of sessions, the problem, experience, travel and follow-up. Ask exactly what is included before you pay.
? At what age should dog training start?
On the first day at home, with short reward-based sessions. Puppy classes usually start after the first vaccination with your vet's agreement. Adult dogs learn at any age.
? How many sessions with a dog trainer will I need?
It depends on the goal. Basic courses are often a handful of weekly sessions; fear and aggression take longer.
? Is a group class or in-home training better?
Group classes suit puppies, calm dogs and basic skills. For barking, toilet problems or fear of other dogs, private or in-home sessions usually work better.
? Is board-and-train a good idea?
It carries real risks because you cannot see the methods. If you choose it, visit first and insist on handover sessions with you.
:::
