:::summary
**Pet insurance** pays part of your dog's or cat's vet bills after an accident, illness or surgery, and sometimes covers liability to other people, in return for a regular premium. Most policies exclude pre-existing conditions, vaccinations and routine care, and come with waiting periods, an excess and limits. Because wording differs between policies, read the full policy document before you buy and ask the insurer about anything that is unclear.

One evening your cat swallows a piece of string, or your dog slips on the stairs, and the vet bill is far bigger than you expected. Pet insurance exists for moments like these: a regular, predictable cost instead of a sudden, heavy one. Policies vary a lot in what they cover and on what terms, so understanding the building blocks is worth more than any advert. This guide explains the jargon in plain language, without naming any insurer, so you can judge policies for yourself. You can also read an overview of the options on [opetlife's pet insurance page](/en/insurance).

## What is pet insurance and how does it work?

You pay a premium monthly or yearly. If your pet has a covered accident or illness while the policy is active, you usually pay the vet first and then claim back part of the cost from the insurer with the right documents. A few terms to know before you compare anything:

- **Policy wording:** the contract itself, including the general and specific conditions and the list of exclusions.
- **Premium:** what you pay for the cover.
- **Waiting period:** the time between the start date and when cover actually begins. It is usually shorter for accidents than for illness and some surgeries.
- **Excess (deductible):** the part of each claim you pay yourself, either a fixed amount or a percentage of the bill.
- **Limit:** the most the insurer will pay per year, per condition or per incident.

## What does dog and cat insurance usually cover?

Most dog and cat policies are built from a few blocks. One policy may offer a single block, another several:

- **Accident:** sudden injuries such as fractures, wounds, road accidents or swallowing a foreign object. Some policies also treat poisoning as an accident; know the [toxic foods for dogs](/en/blog/toxic-foods-for-dogs) so it never comes to that.
- **Illness:** infections and digestive, skin or urinary problems. Some policies cover long-term conditions only up to a set amount or for a set time.
- **Surgery and hospital stays:** operations, anaesthesia, hospitalisation and aftercare. This is where insurance shows its value most, because these costs are large and sudden.
- **Third-party liability:** mostly for dogs, if your dog injures someone or damages someone else's property. Depending on where you live, this may be part of a pet policy, a separate policy or part of home insurance.
- **Sometimes death or theft:** some policies pay a sum if your pet dies from an accident or illness, or is stolen.

Which blocks a policy includes, and on what conditions, is only ever stated in that policy's wording.

## What is usually not covered?

Exclusions are the most important part of any policy, and most disputes start here. Common ones:

- **Pre-existing conditions:** problems that showed signs before the policy started or during the waiting period.
- **Routine and preventive care:** vaccinations, parasite control, annual check-ups and routine dental cleaning. Some insurers sell these as a separate add-on.
- **Neutering, breeding, pregnancy and birth.**
- **Cosmetic procedures** such as ear cropping or tail docking.
- **Sometimes hereditary or congenital conditions, behaviour problems and dental treatment,** with terms that vary from policy to policy.
- **Food and supplements,** including prescription diets.

Not being covered is no reason to skip vaccines or parasite control; some policies even require vaccinations to be up to date. Keep your [cat vaccination schedule](/en/blog/cat-vaccination-schedule) on track, and remember that parasite prevention protects your family too, as our guide to [zoonotic diseases from pets](/en/blog/pet-zoonoses-health-risks) explains.

:::warning
In an emergency, treatment comes first: don't wait for an insurer's approval. Difficulty breathing, heavy bleeding, seizures, sudden collapse, eating poison or medication, a swollen belly with unproductive retching, and a cat straining without passing urine all mean go to a vet now. Before anything happens, [find a vet](/en/vet/find) near you and save the number of a [24-hour emergency vet](/en/blog/24-hour-vet-guide). The mistakes that sink claims: not disclosing past health problems when you buy, missing the claim deadline and not keeping invoices and vet reports.
:::

## What does the premium depend on?

Prices vary by country and change over time, so instead of a number, here are the factors that drive them:

- **Species and breed:** dog or cat, and breeds prone to certain conditions.
- **Age at the start:** older pets usually cost more to insure, and some policies have an upper age limit for new customers.
- **Health history,** and whether a vet check is needed before cover starts.
- **Where you live:** vet fees are not the same everywhere.
- **Level of cover:** accident only, accident and illness, or with liability and add-ons.
- **Excess and limits:** a higher excess usually means a lower premium but a bigger share for you on every claim.
- **How you pay and how many pets** are on one policy.

## Checklist: how to compare pet insurance policies

[[INFOGRAPHIC]]

Put two or three policies side by side and answer these questions for each. Every answer should be in the policy wording, not just the advert:

- Which blocks are included: accident, illness, surgery, liability, death or theft?
- How long is the waiting period for each part?
- How is the excess calculated, and is the limit per year, per condition or per incident?
- Is a long-term condition still covered after renewal, or excluded from the next year?
- What are the age limits for joining and renewing?
- Can you use any vet, or only those the insurer works with?
- What is the deadline for claims, and which documents are needed?
- How long does payment take, and how is it made?
- What are the terms for cancelling, refunds and premium changes at renewal?

Where the wording is unclear, ask the insurer in writing and keep the reply.

## Making a claim: which documents to keep

Many rejected claims come down to missing paperwork or late notice. Set up this routine from day one:

1. Create a paper or digital folder for your pet: vaccination record, microchip number if it has one, and all vet records.
2. Before you buy, book a full check-up so your pet's health at the start of cover is documented.
3. After every visit, ask for an itemised invoice, the diagnosis and treatment notes, any prescription and test results.
4. Tell the insurer about the accident or illness within the stated deadline, and note the date and how you did it.
5. Fill in the claim form completely and keep copies of everything you send.
6. If a claim is rejected, ask for the reason in writing and compare it with the relevant clause in your policy.

## Is pet insurance worth it? Alternatives to consider

Insurance pays off for large, sudden costs, not small predictable ones. If an unexpected surgery bill would put you under real pressure, cover can buy peace of mind. If your pet is young and healthy and you could absorb a surprise bill, a simpler option may suit you better.

**A pet savings pot:** put a fixed amount into a separate account every month and use it only for your pet's care. The money stays yours, but in the first years it may not be enough for major surgery. Some owners combine both: a basic policy for accidents and surgery, and the savings pot for vaccines and routine check-ups. Either way, decide on the basis of the policy wording, not an advert or someone else's experience.

## Frequently asked questions

:::faq
? What is pet insurance?
A contract that, in return for a premium, pays part of your dog's or cat's vet costs after an accident, illness or surgery, and sometimes covers liability to others. Exact cover, waiting periods, excess and limits differ between policies.
? Is pet insurance worth it?
It can be if a large, sudden vet bill would be hard for you to pay. If not, a monthly savings pot is a good alternative, and some owners use both.
? What does pet insurance usually not cover?
Usually pre-existing conditions, vaccinations and parasite control, routine check-ups, neutering, pregnancy and cosmetic procedures. The exact exclusions are only found in the policy wording.
? What is a waiting period in pet insurance?
The time between the policy start and when cover begins. An illness that starts during this time is usually not covered, and the period may differ for accidents and illness.
? What affects the cost of pet insurance?
Species and breed, age, health history, where you live, the level of cover, the excess and the limits. More cover and a lower excess usually mean a higher premium.
? Can I insure an older dog or cat?
Often yes, though some policies have age limits for new customers and older pets usually cost more to insure. Check the age terms for both joining and renewal.
:::

Pet insurance does not replace good care, but it can separate hard treatment decisions from money worries. Learn the building blocks, read the exclusions carefully and keep your records in order.
