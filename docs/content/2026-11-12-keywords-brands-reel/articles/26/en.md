Good **dog and cat boarding** is a place you have seen in person: it is clean, cool and well ventilated, keeps dogs and cats apart, has staff on site at night, works with a named vet and asks every guest for up-to-date vaccination records. Book a trial night before a long trip, pack your pet's usual food and any medication with written instructions, and for cats, seniors or anxious pets, consider a pet sitter in your own home instead.

Sooner or later, a holiday or a work trip raises the question of where your pet will stay. Dog and cat boarding standards vary enormously, and a glossy website or the words "pet hotel" tell you very little. You can find and compare [pet boarding providers on opetlife](/en/services/BOARDING); this guide shows you what to check on a visit, what to ask and how to prepare your pet.

## Boarding kennel, home boarding or pet sitter: which is best?

The right choice depends on species, age, health, temperament and the length of your trip:

- **Boarding kennel, cattery or pet hotel:** a dedicated facility with several staff and a separate space for each animal. It suits sociable, energetic dogs that don't cope well alone.
- **Home boarding:** a carer looks after a few pets in their own home. It's calmer, but check for separate spaces, secure doors and windows and a clear routine.
- **Pet sitter in your home:** a sitter visits once or twice a day or stays overnight. Your pet keeps its own territory and meets no strange animals, often the better choice for cats and for elderly or anxious pets. You can compare [pet sitters on opetlife](/en/services/SITTING).
- **Taking your pet along:** if your accommodation accepts pets and your pet travels well, you may not need boarding at all.

A dog that barks for hours when left alone usually does better with staff around all day than with a sitter who drops in twice. If that's your dog, read [how to stop a dog barking](/en/blog/stop-dog-barking) and tell the provider.

## Dog and cat boarding checklist: what to look for on a visit

Always visit before you book, and ask to see where the animals sleep, not just the reception.

### Space, cleanliness and security

- A faint animal smell is normal; a strong smell of urine or ammonia is not.
- Each animal has a clean, dry sleeping area big enough to stand, turn and lie down.
- Water bowls are full and clean, and mess is picked up promptly.
- Fences are solid, with a second door or closed corridor so no animal can slip out.
- Group play is supervised, and dogs are grouped by size and temperament.

### Heat and ventilation

Overheating is a major summer risk, especially for flat-faced breeds such as pugs and bulldogs and for older pets. Look for good airflow or air conditioning, shade and fresh water, and ask what happens in a power cut. In winter, sleeping areas should be warm and draught-free.

### Dogs and cats kept apart

Cats belong in a separate area, away from barking and the smell of dogs, and cats from different households shouldn't share space. A hiding box, a high shelf and a litter tray for each cat show that a cattery understands cats.

### Nights and vet care

Ask whether someone stays overnight, which vet practice they use, how they would get your pet there in an emergency and whether they will call you before any treatment.

## Questions to ask before you book

Get the answers, ideally in writing:

- How often and how long will my dog be walked or played with?
- Will you feed our own food, in the same amounts and at the same times?
- Who gives medication, and is it recorded?
- If my pet stops eating, coughs or gets hurt, how soon will you tell me?
- Is there a booking form with emergency contacts and consent for vet treatment?
- Are you licensed or registered, where that is required locally?

Red flags: refusing to show sleeping areas, overcrowding, vague answers, promises that "nothing ever goes wrong" and sedatives offered without a vet's prescription.

## What vaccinations does my pet need for boarding?

Where many animals share a space, infections such as kennel cough in dogs and cat flu spread easily, so reputable providers ask every guest for valid vaccination records. If they don't ask you, they haven't asked anyone else. Exactly which vaccines are required depends on the provider's rules and your local vet's advice; some also ask for a kennel cough vaccine. Get the list in writing and talk to your vet a few weeks before you travel, in case a booster is needed. For cats, our [cat vaccination schedule](/en/blog/cat-vaccination-schedule) explains the basics.

Parasite control matters too, because fleas and ticks pass easily between guests; see [flea and tick prevention](/en/blog/flea-tick-prevention). Don't board a pet that is already coughing or unwell, and tell the provider in advance if your female dog is in season.

## Cat boarding: stress and the option of staying home

Cats are territorial, and a new place is usually harder on them than on dogs. Hiding, not eating and avoiding the litter tray are common signs of stress. A cat that hasn't eaten for more than 24 hours should be checked by a vet, because prolonged fasting can cause serious liver problems in cats, so ask the cattery to tell you the same day if your cat doesn't eat.

For most cats, a sitter at home is the least stressful option. If you choose a cattery, ask for a cat-only area and the same litter and food, and leave the carrier in the pen as a familiar hiding place.

## From trial night to pickup: step by step

1. Book early; good places fill up fast around school holidays.
2. Before a long trip, book a trial night and see how your pet comes home.
3. Write a one-page sheet: food, medication, habits, fears, your number, an emergency contact and your vet.
4. Pack everything on the list below and label it.
5. Travel calmly, cats always in a carrier, and in hot weather during the cooler hours. No car? Our [pet taxi guide](/en/blog/pet-taxi-guide) can help.
6. Keep goodbyes short; long, emotional farewells make pets more anxious.

### What to pack for boarding

- Your pet's usual food for every day plus a little extra; a sudden change can upset the stomach.
- Medication in its original packaging, with written instructions.
- A copy of the vaccination record and your vet's details.
- A collar or harness with an ID tag.
- A blanket or worn T-shirt that smells of home.
- For cats: the litter they use at home.

Back home, let your pet rest on its usual food. Tiredness, extra thirst and slightly soft stools from excitement usually settle quickly. If they don't, or there is blood, read about [diarrhoea in dogs](/en/blog/dog-diarrhea-causes) and call your vet.

:::warning
See a vet the same day after boarding for a persistent cough, nasal or eye discharge, lethargy, repeated vomiting, bloody diarrhoea, limping or bite wounds, or refusal to eat for more than a day (more than 24 hours in a cat). Difficulty breathing, pale gums or collapse are emergencies: go immediately. If your pet falls ill during the stay and the provider hasn't contacted you or a vet, collect your pet early.
:::

## How much does dog and cat boarding cost?

Prices vary by place and season, so compare providers on what drives the cost:

- **Location:** city and rural providers often price differently.
- **Accommodation:** a kennel run, a private room or home boarding.
- **Size and needs:** large dogs need more space; elderly or ill pets need more care.
- **Length of stay and season:** holidays are the busiest times.
- **Extras:** extra walks, a bath before pickup, medication, special diets or pick-up and drop-off.
- **Supervision:** overnight staff and quick access to a vet.

Ask for a written list of what's included and what costs extra. The cheapest option isn't always good value, and the most expensive guarantees nothing. A pet sitter's fee mostly depends on the number and length of visits and overnight stays.

## Dog and cat boarding: frequently asked questions

:::faq
? How much does boarding a dog cost?
There is no fixed price. It depends on location, accommodation type, your dog's size, length of stay, season and extras. Ask several providers for a written breakdown and compare quality, not just price.
? Is a cattery or a pet sitter better for my cat?
For most cats, staying home with a trusted sitter is less stressful. If your cat is ill or needs regular medication, a cattery with a cat-only area and quick vet access may be safer.
? What vaccines are required for boarding?
It depends on each provider's rules and your local vet's advice. Ask for the list in writing and see your vet a few weeks before your trip.
? Will my dog be stressed in boarding?
Some anxiety at first is normal. Familiar food, a blanket from home, a short goodbye and a trial night help. If your dog stops eating or seems lethargic, the provider should tell you.
:::

Choose a boarding place with your own eyes, not from photos. Book early, have vaccine and parasite records ready, start with a trial night, and for cats or anxious pets, consider care at home.
